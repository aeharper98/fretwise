# Hear the home, then number everything

Fretwise should teach theory as one numbered language (scale degrees and chord numbers heard against a tonic) that the learner hears, finds on the fretboard and uses straight away in real roots tunes, rather than drilling it as a separate subject. The core for jamming, soloing, learning by ear and songwriting is small: the tonic, the major scale and its pentatonic as numbers, chord tones, I–IV–V function and jam-song form, three "off-chords" (the 6m, the major 2 that is "the 5 of the 5", and the ♭7), Mixolydian and Dorian colour, the ♭3-to-3 blue note, blues forms and circle-of-fifths chains; key signatures, isolated interval naming and abstract chord-quality labels can wait. The path should grow outward from home in eight phases: from the first week the learner hears the tonic and follows I–IV–V in time while note anchors, octave shapes and the capo build the fretboard map; chord tones, top-three-string triads and melody-first breaks come next, then off-chords and modes, closed positions, blues and circle chains, with songwriting alongside and jazz-leaning harmony as an optional bridge. Teaching should start from the melody and from sound, with answers played on the guitar into the microphone, because the link from ear to hand belongs to each instrument and playing by ear is the skill most closely tied to improvising. Stages should stop passing on streaks such as "15 in a row", which at 80% accuracy takes about 137 attempts on average and can leave most of a stage untested; instead each item should be judged on accuracy plus speed, stages should unlock at a moderate bar, and "mastered" should require spaced re-checks on later days, fed by a per-item review queue. Pianists should test out of concepts but never out of fretboard mapping, technique or ear-to-guitar work. The learning-science principles behind this design are strong, but guitar-specific research is nearly absent and no study validates any full sequence, so the order below is a defensible default for Fretwise's own data to tune.

## About a dozen numbered ideas carry roots jamming and writing

Roots music runs on a few reusable chord templates, which is why a small theory core goes a long way. Pete Wernick's method is the main structured way bluegrass jamming is taught. He observes that jam songs usually begin on the 1, reliably resolve to it, **almost always put the 5 just before the end** and close with a 1–5–1 line. Many reuse one four-line cycle for verse and chorus, so a whole song compresses into a string like "4151 twice" ([Wernick, Jamming Fundamentals](https://www.deeringbanjos.com/blogs/deering-banjos-blog/jamming-fundamentals-chord-changing-made-easy)). His method starts with two-chord I–V songs such as "Handsome Molly" and "John Henry" before moving to three-chord songs ([Wernick Method](https://wernickmethod.org/jam-resources/two-chord-songs)). An Idaho jam teacher catalogues standard I–IV–V cycles under codes. He charts "Nine Pound Hammer" as 1144 1511 and "Will the Circle Be Unbroken" as 1111 4411 1111 1511 ([IBA](https://www.idahobluegrassassociation.org/jasons-beginner-jam-blog-2021---2023/nine-pound-hammer); [IBA](https://www.idahobluegrassassociation.org/jasons-beginner-jam-blog-2019---2021/june-04th-2019)). At this base level roots and pop agree: in Hooktheory's analysis of about 1,300 popular songs, I, IV and V are the most frequent chords ([Hooktheory](https://www.hooktheory.com/blog/i-analyzed-the-chords-of-1300-popular-songs-for-patterns-this-is-what-i-found/)). Taken together, the sources suggest that **about ten progression families cover most jam material**.

| # | Family | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Two-chord I–V songs | "Handsome Molly", "John Henry" |
| 2 | Four-line I–IV–V cycles | "Nine Pound Hammer", "Will the Circle Be Unbroken" |
| 3 | The 6m substituted for the 1 | "Will the Circle" as often played at jams |
| 4 | The major 2 chord leading to the 5 | "Red Wing", "Old Home Place" |
| 5 | VI7–II7–V7–I | "Salty Dog Blues", "Sweet Georgia Brown" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweet_Georgia_Brown)) |
| 6 | The ragtime chain III7–VI7–II7–V7–I | Ragtime and swing tunes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ragtime_progression)) |
| 7 | I–♭VII (Mixolydian) | "Old Joe Clark", "Salt Creek" |
| 8 | Dorian i–♭VII | Modal "Shady Grove", "Cluck Old Hen" |
| 9 | Minor i–iv–V | "Wayfaring Stranger" (online charts conflict) |
| 10 | Blues forms | Nine-bar "Sitting on Top of the World" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sitting_on_Top_of_the_World)); old-time versions that drop bars ([Ukulele Magazine](https://ukulelemagazine.com/lessons/learn-to-fingerpick-deep-ellum-blues-on-ukulele)) |

Beyond I–IV–V, roots repertoire has priorities that pop statistics would miss. The same Idaho teacher names **the major 2 chord and the ♭7 as the two most common "off-chords" in traditional bluegrass**. He explains the 2 as "the 5 of the 5", which normally leads to the 5 ([IBA](https://www.idahobluegrassassociation.org/jasons-intermediate-jam-blog-2017---2018/previous/2)). Jams often put a 6m in place of the 1 in "Will the Circle" ([IBA](https://www.idahobluegrassassociation.org/jasons-beginner-jam-blog-2019---2021/june-04th-2019)). "Salty Dog Blues" runs the full VI7–II7–V7–I chain, E–A–D–G in G ([IBA](https://www.idahobluegrassassociation.org/intermediate-jam-blog/salty-dog-blues)), although one jam songbook calls that chain rare in bluegrass ([Bruno songbook](https://mattcbruno.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/MattCBruno-Bluegrass-Jam-Songbook-v1.3.pdf)). The ♭VII chord gives "Old Joe Clark", "Salt Creek", "Red Haired Boy" and "Cripple Creek" their Mixolydian flavour ([Pickers' Guide](https://pickersguide.com/bluegrass-101/the-music/drive-harmony-and-form/)). Some of the oldest old-time fiddle tunes are Mixolydian, Dorian or Aeolian ([e-WV](https://wvencyclopedia.org/entries/2450)).

Versions of the same song differ, so learners must hear and adapt rather than memorize one correct chart. "Shady Grove" is a two-chord I–V song on Wernick's list ([Wernick Method](https://wernickmethod.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/2-Chord-Songs.pdf)) but an Am–G Dorian tune in old-time playing ([Fiddle Hangout](https://www.fiddlehangout.com/archive/53361)). Forum posters report that Bill Monroe recorded "Old Joe Clark" with the V chord where later players use ♭VII ([Banjo Hangout](https://www.banjohangout.org/archive/269908)).

One correction follows for the current build. In the Nashville Number System a bare number means a major chord and a dash marks minor ([Wikipedia: Nashville Number System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nashville_Number_System); [Songcage](https://songcage.com/blog/nashville-number-system/)). So the bluegrass "2" is the major II, yet the Learn stage "Add the 2 and 6 minors" teaches the 2 only as minor. **The path needs both the "2-" (minor ii) and the "2" or "2⁷" (the V of V), named as different chords with different jobs.**

The theory needed for soloing is just as lean, because a bluegrass break varies the tune rather than replacing it. Teachers start lead playing on **the root of each chord, then its other chord tones** ([Bluegrass Unlimited](https://bluegrassunlimited.com/?p=18233)). They build lines of 1, 2 or 4 bars that end on chord tones, aim for the root and 3rd of the IV when the chord moves from I to IV, and hold notes that both chords share across the change ([Peghead Nation handout](https://www.pegheadnation.com/explore/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/781595518583_Flatpicking_Lesson_Improvising2.pdf)). The style's signature colour is the major pentatonic plus a ♭3 that slides into the 3 ([ArtistWorks](https://artistworks.com/go/bluegrass-guitar-scales); [Premier Guitar](https://www.premierguitar.com/articles/28225-twang-101-the-bakersfield-sound)). The ♭7 supplies the Mixolydian sound of the modal fiddle tunes. Neighbour tones and chromatic passing tones connect the target notes. Guide tones, the 3 and ♭7 of each dominant chord, carry the same idea into blues and swing ([Andersen 2010](https://pasquin.uv.es/public/journals/35/pdfs/andersen10.pdf)). One set of numbers for both melody notes and chords ("land on the 3 of the 4 chord") keeps ear, fretboard and harmony in a single vocabulary that needs no staff notation.

Songwriting adds a vocabulary of stability and variation to the same core. Pat Pattison judges each section of a song by how stable or unstable its idea, melody, rhythm, chords and lyric structure feel ([Pattison course notes](https://jenniferpotterblog.wordpress.com/2013/03/17/week-2-of-pat-pattisons-songwriting-course-and-sac-blogging-challenge/)). One critic calls those claims "very subjective" ([notes on Writing Better Lyrics](https://pdfcoffee.com/writing-better-lyrics-notes-on-pattisonx27s-book-oxford-songwriting-pdf-free.html)). Still, the idea maps neatly onto harmony: a verse that asks can end on the 5, and a chorus that answers lands on the 1. Jimmy Kachulis's Berklee harmony text teaches the common progressions and ways to modify them ([Groove3](https://www.groove3.com/products/The-Songwriters-Workshop-Harmony)). Open Music Theory's templates (I–vi–IV–V, the Mixolydian ♭VII–IV–I and the nostalgic IV–iv–I) supply pop contrasts ([OMT](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/4-chord-schemas/); [OMT modal schemas](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/modal-schemas/)).

The bridge to wider musicianship runs through chains roots players already meet. The ragtime chain sounds "harder, bouncier" than its diatonic cousin vi–ii–V7–I ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ragtime_progression)). So turning "Salty Dog" into vi7–ii7–V7–Imaj7 introduces ii–V–I, the core jazz progression, as a softer version of a sound the learner knows. Major-7th, minor-7th and half-diminished chords matter for swing, newgrass and general fluency, but traditional jamming does not require them.

Several staples of conventional theory courses earn little here. Key signatures matter mainly to people who read notation. Forum musicians report that hand signals for a song's key are rarer than chord-number signals, because many guitar and bass players do not know key signatures well ([Steel Guitar Forum](https://bb.steelguitarforum.com/viewtopic.php?p=474200)). The sharps count can ride along with the circle of fifths. **Naming intervals in isolation transfers weakly.** Gary Karpinski found little evidence linking interval identification out of context to the same skill inside a key ([Chenette, Music Theory Online](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.21.27.2/mto.21.27.2.chenette.pdf)). Non-musicians' thresholds for telling intervals apart often exceed a semitone ([McDermott et al.](https://www.academia.edu/17797938/Musical_intervals_and_relative_pitch_Frequency_resolution_not_interval_resolution_is_special)). Abstract labels for chord quality are hard for untrained adults. Non-musicians who sorted melodies correctly 73% of the time with happy/sad labels managed only 65% with major/minor labels, even after some training ([Halpern et al.](https://core.ac.uk/works/84164274)). In another study, roughly 70% of listeners showed very little sensitivity to the difference between major and minor ([Chubb et al. 2013](https://cwlab.ss.uci.edu/files/2014/01/ChubbEtAl2013.pdf)).

Intervals still belong in the course, but taught when needed and tied to a job: 3rds explain chord quality, 4ths and 5ths explain root motion and the boom-chuck bass, the ♭7 explains dominant chords and ♭VII, and the half step explains why V7 resolves. That order matches JustinGuitar's theory course, which formalizes intervals only after the major scale and triads ([JustinGuitar](https://www.justinguitar.com/classes/practical-music-theory-course-fast-hands-on)). No bluegrass source surfaced that teaches three-notes-per-string scales, a system built for fast alternate-picked runs in rock and fusion ([The Acoustic Guitarist](https://theacousticguitarist.com/alternatives-to-the-caged-system/)). It belongs in an optional late module.

## Eight phases grow outward from home, one key at a time

The structure that best fits the evidence has three strands that advance together in the same key: fretboard and hands, ear, and harmony and melody. A lesson on the notes of G major pairs with an ear drill on scale degrees in G on the same frets. A lesson on the G, C and D shapes pairs with following I–IV–V in G. Each phase ends in an applied checkpoint that the learner picks by goal: jam and solo, learn by ear, or write songs. Music students whose needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness are met report more self-driven motivation to practise ([Evans & Bonneville-Roussy 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735615610926)). Pacing follows each item's mastery, not the calendar.

The current Learn path runs the other way: twelve stages of note names, mostly one string at a time, before the first chord. The Wernick Method, by contrast, has students jamming in the first hour. All they need is to tune with an electronic tuner and change smoothly among G, C, D and A ([Wernick Method FAQs](https://wernickmethod.org/faqs)). The table sets out the recommended phases, and the reasons for the order follow it.

| Phase | Theory (as numbers) | Fretboard and hands | Ear (answered on the guitar) | Applied checkpoint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Home and three chords | Tonic as home; the key as a 1-4-5 family; 1 is home, 4 lifts, 5 pulls; jam-song form (start and end on 1, 5 before the end) | Open strings and first-position natural notes, with the B–C, E–F and fifth-fret rules; strings 6 and 5 as home strings; G, C, D, then A and E; boom-chuck root, then alternating bass | "Find home" after a cadence; "Bass on One" over I–V, then I–IV–V, at 60–75 bpm | Two-chord, then three-chord songs in a simulated slow jam |
| 2. Numbers in the hand | Major scale as whole and half steps (W-W-H-W-W-W-H); triad quality on each degree (1, 4, 5 major; 2, 3, 6 minor; 7 diminished); number tables for G, C, D, A, E; a capo keeps the numbers and moves the key | Octave shapes in place of rote learning of strings 4–1; "find every C"; G and D major in first position; capo translator | Degrees 1, 5, 3 with cadence and resolution, then add 2 and 6; echoes of 2–4-note motifs; vi heard as the "darker" chord | Sing, find and play a simple melody as numbers; chart a song heard by ear as number strings |
| 3. Chord tones and first breaks | Chord tones 1-3-5 (and the ♭7 of V7) landed on downbeats and changes; notes shared across a change; licks placed in the song form, starting with the G run | Top-three-string triads of I, IV, V in G in three inversions, then C, D, A; chord changes using the closest voicing | Learn-a-tune-by-ear workflow; "play the third"; call and response with a widening pool of allowed notes | Skeleton break, then embellished break; fake a break over an unfamiliar song; pass with notice |
| 4. Off-chords and roots colour | 6m in place of 1; major 2 or 2⁷ as the 5 of the 5; ♭VII and Mixolydian; Dorian i–♭VII; minor keys; ♭3→3 and ♭7 blue notes; neighbour and passing tones | Double stops taken from the triads; major pentatonic plus ♭3; "mode switch" (lower the 7th, then the 3rd) | Full major scale (add 4 and 7), then ♭7, ♭3 and modal tonics; spot the off-chord; compare two versions of one tune | "Will the Circle" with the 6m, "Red Wing", "Old Joe Clark" with V and with ♭VII, modal "Shady Grove" |
| 5. Up the neck through tunes | Positions as the homes of chord shapes; three movable shapes per chord; CAGED as the map that joins them | Closed positions (G at frets 5 and 7, C at 3, D two frets above C); Sutton- and Miller-style shapes, then CAGED with the G and D forms last; melody on one string; 3-3-2 crosspicking | One phrase in low, middle and high registers; licks moved to new keys | "Blackberry Blossom", "Forked Deer", "Arkansas Traveler"; a dressed-up second break |
| 6. Blues and circle chains | Dominant 7ths on 1, 4, 5; 12-, 8- and 9-bar forms and loose old-time forms; VI7–II7–V7–I and III7–VI7–II7–V7–I; circle of fifths as a map; guide tones 3 and ♭7 | Seventh-chord shapes and arpeggios; guide-tone lines that leap within a chord and step across changes | Count the form; target the 3rd of each chord through a circle chain | "Salty Dog", "Sitting on Top of the World", "Deep Elem Blues" |
| 7. Writing with harmony (runs alongside from phase 2) | Stable versus unstable endings; 6–10 templates; variation moves (harmonic rhythm, substitution, pedal, borrowed iv and ♭VII); modulation | Chord builder and capo helper to set a song in the singer's key | "Stable or unstable?"; predict the next chord from how often it follows in roots songs | Rewrite a I–IV–V verse with one move; chart an original song in numbers |
| 8. Bridge to general musicianship (optional) | Major versus minor pentatonic over blues; swing feel; diatonic 7ths; ii–V–I as the diatonic version of the circle chain; modes as a system | Positions that mix the two pentatonics; 7th-chord voicings; three-notes-per-string only for learners who want fast scale runs | Hear the pentatonic colour change chord by chord; swing versus straight eighths | "Minor Swing", Western-swing backup, a 12-bar that switches colour by chord |

Home comes first because every other skill is defined against it. In melodic dictation the tonic was the least-missed scale degree ([Paney, Texas Tech](https://ttu-ir.tdl.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/688ad13a-e2b0-41fc-953b-5a8c089644a4/content)), and guides to finding a song's key begin by locating it ([UEN Aural Skills](https://uen.pressbooks.pub/auralskills/chapter/listening-for-key/)). Wernick singles out hearing chord changes as the main hurdle for many jammers. He advises learning the 1, 4 and 5 of each key and strumming along with familiar songs ([Wernick, Deering blog](https://blog.deeringbanjos.com/a-super-useful-jam-skill-hearing-chord-changes)). Following changes therefore starts in week one, at the 60–75 bpm Wernick uses for beginners ([Wernick Method](https://wernickmethod.org/bluegrass-jamming-basics)). The learner proves each change by playing **the new chord's root on beat 1**. That is the bass note boom-chuck playing already uses, and a single pitch the microphone can score. At first the next chord's number appears a bar early, as a jam leader would signal; later rounds fade the cue.

Note learning shrinks to a few anchors plus transfer rules. Teachers converge on natural notes first, the 6th and 5th strings as home strings, landmark frets, the fifth-fret unison rule and octave shapes. They call memorizing every note one by one "a classic beginner's trap" ([Riffhard](https://www.riffhard.com/how-to-memorize-the-guitar-fretboard/)). JustinGuitar teaches note finding over the whole neck through octave shapes ([JustinGuitar community, IM-116](https://community.justinguitar.com/t/notes-on-the-whole-fretboard/2013)). The only controlled comparison found, Amy Brandon's 14-person pilot study, showed **no advantage for any memorization method** ([The Scientific Guitarist](https://thescientificguitarist.wordpress.com/current-studies/)). Methods should therefore be chosen by how well they feed later skills. Octave shapes win that test. They follow from standard tuning. From the 6th or 5th string, the octave is two strings over and two frets up. From the 4th or 3rd string it is three frets up, because the B string breaks the tuning pattern by one fret. Octave shapes can replace the rote stages for strings 4 to 1 while preparing movable chords and triads.

The bass strings also hold the I–IV–V. Forum posts describe JustinGuitar's "Big Six" as the natural notes at frets 3, 5 and 7 of strings 5 and 6, which form the chain C–G–D–A–E–B ([JustinGuitar community](https://community.justinguitar.com/t/the-big-six-essential-notes/1751)). Any three neighbours in that chain are the IV, I and V of the middle note. So the I, IV and V bass roots of G, D, A and E all lie within frets 3–7.

The capo comes before playing up the neck, because it is how bluegrass usually changes key. Tony Rice frequently played from open G shapes and used a capo for other keys. "Red Haired Boy" in A, for example, he played with the capo at the 2nd fret ([Vintage Guitar](https://www.vintageguitar.com/38362/fretprints-tony-rice/)). Common practice is capo 2 for A or D, 3 for B♭ and 4 for B or E ([Flatpicker Hangout](https://www.flatpickerhangout.com/archive/27357)). The capo also teaches the deepest idea in the number system, that the numbers stay put while the key moves, and it ties straight into the app's capo helper.

Movable shapes then arrive as triads, before full CAGED. Bryan Sutton's 3 Shape Method organizes the neck around three chord shapes "transposed into any key, and utilized in any register" ([ArtistWorks](https://blog.artistworks.com/bluegrass-guitar-lesson-3-shape-method/)), and Dan Miller's *Flatpicking Essentials* builds its up-the-neck road maps on F-, D- and Upper-A-shape "chord centers" ([flatpick.com](https://www.flatpick.com/product_p/2449.htm)). Peghead Nation's advanced flatpicking course teaches root-position and inverted triads on the top three strings ([Peghead Nation](https://www.pegheadnation.com/string-school/courses/advanced-flatpicking-guitar)). Berklee frames its triad course as focusing "less on the shape of an individual chord, and more on the notes" ([Berklee Online](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/guitar-chords-101-triad-inversions-up-the-fretboard/)). Bill Edwards, whose *Fretboard Logic* is a CAGED method, concedes that beginners cannot yet manage the G and D forms higher up the neck ([Bill Edwards](https://billedwards.com/publishing.html)). Triad teachers put the relationship bluntly: "triads are what make CAGED work" ([triads-guitar.com](https://triads-guitar.com/learn/what-are-triads)).

Closed scale positions come next, learned through tunes: G positions at the 7th and 5th frets, C at the 3rd, and D two frets above C, practised in tunes such as "Blackberry Blossom" and "Forked Deer" ([Peghead Nation](https://pegheadnation.com/string-school/courses/advanced-flatpicking-guitar/moving-neck-part-3-keys-c-and-d/)). Sutton warns of "pitfalls for flatpickers, like blues players always using the same scale shapes" ([Fretboard Journal](https://shop.fretboardjournal.com/blogs/dispatch/from-the-archives-bryan-suttons-fretboard-journal-cover-story)). That is why the melody, not a box, should pick the position. Fiddle tunes are typically 32 bars in AABB form ([Premier Guitar](https://www.premierguitar.com/articles/Acoustic_Adventures_Flatpicking_Fiddle_Tunes_Inside_The_Cuckoos_Nest)) and carry technique and positions. Breaks on sung songs carry ear skills and embellishment. Both tracks run from phase 3 on, with crosspicking's syncopated 3-3-2 rolls ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crosspicking)) added in phase 5.

The ear strand grows outward the same way. Degrees 1, 5 and 3 come first, because they are the notes of the I chord the learner already frets. Adding 2 and 6 gives the major pentatonic, which Musical U uses as a stepping stone to the full scale ([Musical U](https://www.musical-u.com/learn/6-simple-steps-play-by-ear-solfa/)). Next come the tendency tones 4 and 7. ♭7, ♭3 and modal tonics arrive with the off-chords that use them. On the harmony side, the learner moves from I–V to I–IV–V, then the vi heard as the "darker" chord, then the 2, ♭VII and 7th chords, always inside songs.

Pentatonic boxes are the most significant thing this order delays, and the alternative deserves a fair hearing. Mainstream courses use the minor pentatonic as the way into improvising; JustinGuitar's first improvisation course is a blues ([JustinGuitar](https://justinguitar.com/guitar-lessons/basic-blues-improvisation-bc-196)). A box gives a beginner something that sounds like a solo within days. For a bluegrass learner, though, that speed builds the wrong habit. An ArtistWorks guide calls reaching for the pentatonic box instead of the song's melody **the biggest mistake in early-intermediate bluegrass breaks** ([ArtistWorks](https://blog.artistworks.com/bluegrass-guitar-breaks-flatpick-melody/)). Even jazz teaching has started beginners on guide tones taken from the chords, before any scale ([Andersen 2010](https://pasquin.uv.es/public/journals/35/pdfs/andersen10.pdf)). The pentatonic therefore enters in phase 4 as colour around melody and chord tones, and in phase 8 as the bridge to blues, where lessons show when to switch between minor and major pentatonic over a 12-bar ([Premier Guitar](https://www.premierguitar.com/lessons/blue-horizons-how-to-mix-major-and-minor-pentatonics)) and crossover tunes such as "Minor Swing", now taught in advanced flatpicking courses ([Peghead Nation](https://pegheadnation.com/string-school/courses/advanced-flatpicking-guitar/djangos-minor-swing-part-1-melody-and-chords)), lead toward swing.

### Firm where teachers agree, provisional where they split

Some parts of the order rest on converging teacher practice: tonic first, following chord changes early, two-chord songs before three-chord songs, breaks built from the melody, and the capo before closed positions. Others are defensible choices that respected teachers would make differently, among them triads before CAGED, degrees 1-5-3 before 1-2-3, the 2 chord before ♭VII, and a cadence rather than a drone to set the key for modal tunes. *Fretboard Logic* and JustinGuitar's CAGED module lead with chord-shape systems ([JustinGuitar CAGED module](https://justinguitar.com/modules/caged-system)). Mick Goodrick starts on a single string, which he calls "the most logical place to begin" ([Fretboard Foundation](https://book.fretboardfoundation.com/single-string.html)). Brandon's verdict that "critically little empirical research has been conducted in guitar skill acquisition" ([UCLA interview](https://ethnomusicologyreview.ucla.edu/content/amy-brandon-scientific-guitarist)) covers every ordering question here. No study compares CAGED, triads, positions and three-notes-per-string.

Two practical cautions apply. The research behind this report was gathered from search-engine summaries of the cited sources rather than full-text reads. Online chord charts for songs such as "Rocky Top" and "Wayfaring Stranger" conflict, and no reliable source surfaced for the exact notes of the G run. Figures, song charts and the G run therefore need checking against primary sources before they reach the app.

## Sound before sight, melody before scales, answers on the guitar

Building breaks from the melody is the closest thing bluegrass teaching has to a consensus. In ArtistWorks' method the learner sings or hums the melody, finds it on the fretboard phrase by phrase, plays it as a plain skeleton, and only then adds slides, hammer-ons, pull-offs and crosspicking. The decorations connect melody notes rather than replace them ([ArtistWorks](https://blog.artistworks.com/bluegrass-guitar-breaks-flatpick-melody/)). The simplest shape for a break is **state the melody, vary it, return to it** ([ArtistWorks](https://blog.artistworks.com/flatpicking-guitar-break-build-any-song)). Steve Kaufman takes each tune through three levels: a basic melody, then an intermediate arrangement, then a full solo ([Mandozine](https://www.mandozine.com/media/CGOW/stevekaufman.html)). Berklee's lesson on folk improvisation centres on finding a melody's essential elements and improvising on them ([Berklee](https://college.berklee.edu/node/1030819)). David Grier builds solos around the song and the space between notes ([Acoustic Shoppe](https://theacousticshoppe.com/a/blog/inside-the-mind-of-david-grier)). Sutton describes improvising as a continuous thread with forward momentum, borrowing "yes, and" from improv theatre ([Acoustic Guitar](https://acousticguitar.com/guitar-great-bryan-sutton-delivers-bluegrass-playing-tips-more/)).

Jam guides give the same advice for live breaks: play close to the melody first, and "dress it up" on the second pass. They also treat passing your turn with notice as normal ([Kansas Prairie Pickers](https://kansas.gov/kppa/p6b_jams_and_jam_etiquette.htm)). In the app, Kaufman's levels become checkable. The level-1 skeleton of strong-beat melody notes is scored note for note. Levels 2 and 3 accept any note from an allowed set and reward chord tones landed on the beat. A simulated jam cues each break with a "nod" a bar or two early and offers a Pass button.

Ear work should start from sound and function: hear a note's role relative to home, then name it, then see it. People tell intervals apart significantly better inside a key than with no key, and this advantage does not depend on years of training ([Frontiers in Psychology 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01753/full)). Karpinski argued that a note's function comes from its scale degree, not from isolated intervals, and that argument underpins the current reform of ear-training courses ([Chenette, Music Theory Online](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.21.27.2/mto.21.27.2.chenette.html)). Musical U, a commercial vendor that still teaches intervals, concedes that "your brain can't recognize intervals quickly enough" to play melodies by ear ([Musical U](https://www.musical-u.com/learn/topic/intervals/)).

The Functional Ear Trainer app plays an I–IV–V–I cadence before each exercise ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1088761926)), and NaturalEar, built on the same method, fades that support over time ([NaturalEar](https://posmlabs.github.io/NaturalEarTrainer/)). Fretwise should copy the cadence-then-fade design and play the correct note resolving to the tonic after every answer. It should also **show fretboard positions only after the answer**, so the sound, not the dots, does the work. Chord quality enters first as "bright or dark" in context, then as a concrete note ("play the third"), because untrained adults handle emotional labels better than major/minor labels.

Answers should be played on the guitar wherever possible, because the link from ear to hand is the skill being trained. In one study, pianists were disrupted only by keyboard sounds and guitarists only by guitar sounds. That is evidence that **learned links between sound and action belong to each instrument** ([Drost, Rieger & Prinz 2007](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17470210601154388)). Musicians who grew up playing by ear played melodies back in fewer tries than formally trained musicians, who spent conscious attention on fingerings ([Woody & Lehmann 2010](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/240725673_Student_Musicians%27_Ear-Playing_Ability_as_a_Function_of_Vernacular_Music_Experiences)). Fretboard fluency is therefore part of ear skill, not a separate module.

Playing by ear in turn drives improvising. In McPherson's model of how musical skills relate, ear playing directly influenced improvising ([McPherson et al. 1997](https://minerva-access.unimelb.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/9cc8eee9-b78d-500a-b164-828edb9384ed/content)). Among 101 high-school wind players, improvisation was "most markedly influenced by an ability to play by ear" ([Woody 2012](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258171046_Playing_by_Ear_Foundation_or_Frill)). In a small case-control study, pupils who learned by copying recordings outperformed matched controls on every post-test measure ([Baker & Green](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/275207584_Ear_playing_and_aural_development_in_the_instrumental_lesson_Results_from_a_case-control_experiment)).

The popular ear-training apps reviewed focus on intervals, chords and progressions, and mostly lack a dedicated scale-degree module ([Sonofield](https://sonofield.com/blog/best-ear-training-apps-2026)). None found answers ear questions by listening to the learner's own guitar, though that search was not exhaustive. That makes answering into the microphone, by playing the degree, root or phrase, Fretwise's clearest teaching advantage.

Notation adds nothing to this loop. Wernick classes use neither sheet music nor tab ([Wernick Method](https://wernickmethod.org/)); Peghead Nation's intermediate bluegrass mandolin course teaches its first five lessons with no tab or notation ([Peghead Nation](https://www.pegheadnation.com/string-school/courses/intermediate-bluegrass-mandolin)); Irish teachers pass tunes on phrase by phrase and move on only when everyone has the phrase ([Irish transmission study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249752549_The_Teacher's_Role_in_Transmission_of_Irish_Traditional_Music)); and the popular musicians in Lucy Green's study learned by copying recordings and working parts out by ear ([Green](https://books.google.com/books/about/How_Popular_Musicians_Learn.html?id=vr-v4sY7MMYC)).

Singing is a useful bridge but a poor gate. Singing a melody back takes fewer tries than playing it ([Woody & Lehmann 2010](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/240725673_Student_Musicians%27_Ear-Playing_Ability_as_a_Function_of_Vernacular_Music_Experiences)), so "hear, hum, find, play" is a sound sequence. Yet roughly **10–15% of untrained adults sing inaccurately even though they hear pitch differences normally**; the fault lies in turning pitch into vocal action ([Pfordresher & Brown](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1425067); [review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3799299)). In a randomized trial, only adults given real-time visual feedback improved their singing ([Psychology of Music](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/03057356211026730)). Hearing a synthesized voice while singing made poor-pitch singers worse ([Pfordresher & Brown 2007](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf)). Singing should therefore stay optional and be scored with a live pitch trace. The app should never play a reference voice over the learner, and should never let singing block progress.

Two cheap design choices multiply what drills achieve. Corrective feedback roughly doubled the benefit of practice testing in Rowland's meta-analysis (effect size g = 0.73 with feedback, 0.39 without; [Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2021-yang.pdf)). And adults learned to identify an interval only when practice alternated with periods of simply listening; continuous practice alone produced no learning ([Little, Cheng & Wright 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6384134)). A minute or two of labelled listening inside each ear block, plus the resolution played after every answer, delivers both.

For harmony, progressions should be taught as named chunks, such as Wernick's "phone number" strings and the Idaho jam's coded templates, and heard bass-first. Karpinski treats hearing the bass line as the foundation of harmonic dictation, and names chunking as one of only two ways to extend short-term memory. In Chenette's small pilot, all but one of 16 nearly correct respondents said they listened to the bass or outer voices ([Chenette, Music Theory Online](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.21.27.2/mto.21.27.2.chenette.html)). A "predict the next chord" exercise can draw on Wernick's predictable forms. Its odds must come from a curated list of roots songs, not from pop corpora.

Learning tunes by ear deserves its own guided workflow, modelled on roots practice: listen several times and hum along, find home, find the first note's degree, loop each phrase at 60–75% tempo and echo it until the microphone confirms it, chain the phrases and raise the tempo, then find the I–IV–V underneath. Old-time slow jams aim to "get the tune in your ears, then under your fingers" ([Fiddle Hangout](https://www.fiddlehangout.com/archive/41112)). Irish-session advice is to build a skeleton of the tune and then vary it ([The Session](https://thesession.org/discussions/43082)).

Stock licks belong in the course too, taught with their place in the song form. Expert improvising is built from patterns: in 48 Charlie Parker solos, **82.6% of notes begin a four-interval pattern that recurs elsewhere** ([Norgaard 2014](https://core.ac.uk/download/71427376.pdf)). The G run, the most popular run in bluegrass ([Wikipedia: G run](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G_run)), is the first lick to learn, as an ending.

Each lesson should run from explicit teaching to use: a worked example the learner both hears and sees, guided practice with hints, independent recall without hints, then use in music. A meta-analysis of 580 comparisons found that unassisted discovery did worse than explicit instruction (d = −0.38). Discovery supported by feedback, worked examples and scaffolding beat other methods (d = 0.30) ([Alfieri et al. 2011](https://physics.uwyo.edu/~rudim/S14_JEduPsych_DoesDiscoveryLrngEnhance.pdf)).

Inside playing tasks, the app should teach the strategies that set apart the best pianists in Duke's study. There, practice time and number of repetitions did not predict retention, but strategy did ([Duke, Simmons & Cash 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429408328851)). On a miss, the app should isolate the failing note or bar, loop it slower, ask for a few clean repetitions and step the tempo back up.

Feedback on breaks should describe the process, as Biasutti recommends for teaching improvisation ([Biasutti 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4426722)): "chord tones landed on six of eight changes", "pulse held through the break". Every break exercise should end with record, listen back and self-rate. Among 73 college jazz players, self-evaluation was the single best predictor of improvisation achievement ([May 2003](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/237969741_Factors_and_Abilities_Influencing_Achievement_in_Instrumental_Jazz_Improvisation)).

Timing is scored in every exercise, not left to a separate rhythm unit. Wernick students begin with simple backup, which builds the rhythm they will later need for soloing ([Graves Mountain](https://www.gravesmountain.com/?p=70412)). Boom-chuck puts a bass note on beats 1 and 3 and a strum on 2 and 4 ([Barnwell, Georgia Tech](https://barnwell.ece.gatech.edu/roles.html)). Standard metronome drills set the click at half tempo, sounding only on beats 2 and 4 ([Premier Guitar](https://www.premierguitar.com/lessons/intermediate/how-to-use-a-metronome)), then drop clicks until the player keeps time alone ([TrueFire](https://learn.truefire.com/metronome-time-signature)). Every new skill should start at about 50–70 bpm on a visible tempo ladder. A break that loses the pulse should score worse than one with a wrong note, because teachers stress keeping a forward pulse for the band even while soloing ([ArtistWorks](https://blog.artistworks.com/bluegrass-guitar-breaks-flatpick-melody/)).

### The microphone hears pitch, not strings

Single-note pitch detection identifies pitch and octave, but not which string played the note, and the same pitch sits on up to three strings within frets 0–12: E4, for example, is the open 1st string, the 2nd string at fret 5 and the 3rd string at fret 9. Exercises that teach a location therefore need tap answers, or a prompt such as "on the 3rd string" that accepts the right pitch; triads and arpeggios can be checked by the microphone as notes played one at a time, in order, in a set register; and chords, double stops and crosspicking need tapping, the chord-shape checker or scoring of note onsets alone. The single-note proofs of hearing are exactly the musical tasks this curriculum needs: the root on beat 1, the third of the current chord, a chord tone on the downbeat, a phrase echoed back.

For showing shapes, the life-size fretboard is the right default view. In two experiments, positions lit up on the guitar itself beat standard diagrams, including for retention two weeks later ([Keebler et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4034341)). Whether a phone screen held beside the guitar gives the same benefit is untested.

## Per-item fluency and spaced relearning should replace fifteen-in-a-row

Fretwise currently passes a stage after 6 to 15 correct answers in a row, and a single miss resets the count. The arithmetic of streaks makes that a noisy gate. Assuming independent answers, reaching 15 in a row takes on average about 23 attempts at 95% accuracy, 39 at 90% and **137 at 80%**, while 8 in a row takes about 10, 13 and 25. Accuracy of 80% is normal for someone meeting new material, so a long streak mostly measures whether the learner avoids slips; it makes stage length unpredictable and punishes the occasional fumble.

It also says little about coverage. "The whole neck" asks for 15 in a row across 78 string-and-fret cells, so a passing run can test **at most 15 of the 78 cells**. With random draws, any given item in a 12-item stage goes untested in about 27% of the shortest passing runs. Simple rules are not the problem in themselves: Pelánek found no fundamental difference between streak rules, moving averages and model-based mastery detectors ([Pelánek](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/nrhm-mastery.pdf)). The problem is what a whole-stage streak measures: accuracy only, in one sitting, for the stage rather than for each item.

Better criteria combine accuracy, speed, coverage and time. Kellman's perceptual-learning modules retire each item once it has been answered correctly several times in a row within a response-time limit. The patent's worked example is three consecutive correct answers, each under five seconds. The rationale is that fast, correct runs show "some degree of automaticity" ([Kellman patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9299265)). Adaptive sequencing was "particularly powerful" when combined with such retirement rules ([Kellman et al. 2008](https://kellmanlab.psych.ucla.edu/files/kellman_et_al_2008.pdf)). In a perceptual-learning study of skin-lesion screening, retirement rules based on accuracy and response time made learning more efficient than accuracy-only mastery, on both immediate and delayed tests ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10764053/)). Speed matters musically too: knowing where the 5 of D is helps only if the hand gets there before the change. Response-time targets must be set per task type from Fretwise's own data, because no validated thresholds exist. Tapping and playing into the microphone, or low and high frets, need different targets.

The bigger change is to separate "unlocked" from "mastered". Successive-relearning research recommends practising to a modest criterion of three correct recalls, then relearning in three widely spaced later sessions ([Rawson & Dunlosky 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09637214221100484)). The advantage of a high first-session criterion **does not persist once relearning happens** ([Vaughn, Dunlosky & Rawson 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-016-0606-y)). A stage should therefore unlock the next one at a moderate in-session bar, and count as "mastered" only after shorter re-checks on later days.

Spacing almost always helps memory, and the best gap grows with how long the knowledge must last. For a test a few weeks away, the best gap is about 20% of that interval; for a test a year away, about 5% ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CVRWP2008.html)). Physical skills behave differently over short spans. Piano novices got no benefit from spacing on a 17-note sequence tested minutes later ([Wiseheart et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5553926/)). Yet accuracy on a keyboard melody improved only across gaps that included sleep ([Simmons & Duke 2006](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002242940605400308)). So licks and chord changes should be judged again the next day rather than drilled to exhaustion in one sitting.

Easily confused categories should be introduced one at a time, with aids, and then mixed. Mixing improved category learning overall (g = 0.42) but backfired for plain word lists (g = −0.39) ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)). For melodic intervals, mixed practice beat one-at-a-time practice only once reference songs and singing were taken away. **Most learners wrongly judged one-at-a-time practice the more effective** ([Wong, Chen & Lim 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735620922595)).

Once each category is known, the app should mix I, IV and V by ear, minor and major thirds, neighbouring triad inversions and the same note on different strings. It should also tell learners briefly why mixed practice feels harder. Feedback should come on every item. First attempts at new material should come with hints: without feedback, testing gave no benefit when initial success was 50% or lower ([Frontiers 2018, citing Rowland](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full)).

The table gives starting rules by item type. The values are starting points to calibrate, not validated thresholds.

| Item type | Checked by | Unlock the next stage | Count as mastered | On a later miss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fretboard facts (fret → name; name → every location; octave partner; capo + shape → key) | Tap for location; microphone for pitch, with an "on string N" prompt | Every item seen; each correct at least twice, the last within its time target; recent accuracy about 85–90% | Passes a five-item probe in 2–3 later sessions on different days | Item returns to the review queue; the stage is not reset |
| Ear categories (degree in a key; chord number; off-chord) | Microphone: play the degree or root in any octave; tap as a quiet fallback | About 85–90% over the last 20 trials, then held with the cadence faded | Same accuracy without aids in later sessions, plus a probe the learner has not met before | Bring back the cadence and resolution for the confused pair |
| Shapes (triad inversions by string set; voicings; double stops) | Tap or chord-shape checker; microphone for notes played one at a time | Each shape named and played quickly 2–3 times | Holds up when keys and string sets are mixed | Re-drill alongside its neighbouring inversions |
| Physical sequences (changes in time; boom-chuck; licks; phrases) | Microphone: note onsets and pitch sequence | Clean repetitions at one tempo step, then the next | Holds its tempo the next day, after sleep | Isolate the failing bar, slow it, climb the tempo ladder again |
| Applied tasks (fake a break; learn a phrase by ear; chart a song) | Process measures plus self-rating | Completion with process targets, such as chord tones on most downbeats and the pulse held | Periodic checkpoints with unseen songs and phrases | Feedback names the process, not only the wrong notes |

Passed material should flow into a per-item review queue rather than whole-stage replays. A Leitner-box scheduler is enough to start: items move between boxes reviewed at lengthening intervals. Duolingo's half-life regression shows where a model-driven version can go. It estimates how long each item stays in each learner's memory, and it cut recall-prediction error by more than 45% compared with baseline models ([Settles & Meeder 2016](https://research.duolingo.com/papers/settles.acl16.pdf)).

Today's practice already counts right answers rather than streaks, and it favours the notes a learner misses. It should extend to every strand, mixing mostly items that are due with current weak items and commonly confused pairs, a few older items and one or two uses in music. Hints should keep success around 75–90%. Stage re-checks should be short probes of about five items. Items that fail go back to relearning, and the stage is never reset.

Mastery also needs a check from outside the drills. Mastery-learning programmes average about half a standard deviation of gain ([Kulik et al. 1990](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/kulik_kulik_Bangert-Drowns_1990.pdf)), but critics argue the gains shrink to near zero on standardized tests ([Slavin](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED294891.pdf)). Computer-based hearing training mostly improves the task that was trained ([PLOS ONE review](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0062836)). Every few weeks, the app should therefore play the learner an unseen four-bar phrase to play back and an unseen three-chord song to follow, and pace the path by the results.

### Short daily sessions, repairable streaks, competence on display

No study fixes an ideal session length for adult guitarists, so 10–20 minutes on most days is a default to test, justified by spacing and sleep rather than by a measured optimum. A session can open with two to four minutes recalling due items, introduce one new concept with a handful of items, move from guided to independent practice, use the concept in music and finish on a success. Within that, an ear block of roughly ten minutes keeps the three strands in the same key: a degree drill, echoes, "Bass on One" and a minute of listening only.

Motivation features should signal competence rather than pressure. Gamification's effects are real but small: g = 0.49 on cognitive outcomes, 0.36 on motivation and 0.25 on behaviour, with the last two less robust in rigorous studies ([Sailer & Homner 2020](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/109056/109056.pdf)). Broken streaks demotivate beyond the lost day. When a broken streak could be repaired, **85% of people continued, against 69% when it could not** ([Silverman & Barasch 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac029); [University of Delaware coverage](https://lerner.udel.edu/seeing-opportunity/lerner-professor-researches-how-streaks-motivate-us/)).

In music, decisions to quit come when the needs for competence, autonomy and relatedness go unmet ([Evans, McPherson & Davidson 2013](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/2013_EvansMcPhersonDavidson_Roleof.pdf)). A model built on those needs explained 45% of the variance in intentions to continue ([Evans & Liu 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0022429418812769)). Fretwise's day streak should therefore offer a repair, count a short review as a day and avoid guilt-based messages. Progress should read as musical competence: "five fiddle tunes jam-ready", "any note on the G string in under two seconds". The jam track should never require soloing to complete, just as the Wernick Method makes soloing optional ([Wernick Method FAQs](https://wernickmethod.org/faqs)).

## Pianists skip concepts, never the fretboard or the hands

No study directly measures how much piano skill carries over to guitar, but three lines of evidence point to the same three-layer model. **Concepts transfer.** Skilled pianists carried learning over to new sequences that kept the same musical structure even when the hand movements changed ([Palmer & Meyer 2000](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/1467-9280.00216)). **The instrument's map does not.** Links between sound and action are specific to a player's own instrument ([Drost, Rieger & Prinz 2007](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17470210601154388)). Pianists have a strong sense of pitch rising from left to right, and that sense is shaped by the instrument played, unlike the sense of pitch rising from low to high in space ([Lega et al. 2020](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/338751880_Instrumental_expertise_and_musical_timbre_modulate_the_spatial_representation_of_pitch); [Timmers & Li](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/99958/3/Timmers%20Li%20Final%20Manuscript.pdf)). **Technique starts near zero.** Fretting needs more pressure than piano's relaxed hand, and sore fingers before calluses form are the main early obstacle ([SWMRS](https://swmrs.com/how-hard-is-learning-guitar-after-piano/)). A pianist therefore arrives knowing far more than their hands, or their ear on the guitar, can yet do. That gap is the main threat to their motivation.

This profile flips the usual priorities. The difficulty pianists report most is finding notes quickly, even when they know the names. A guitar teacher reports that pianists fixate on note names, and get frustrated, instead of learning shapes first. Pianists are also warned off playing "in a straight line" as on a keyboard ([JustinGuitar community](https://community.justinguitar.com/t/musical-background-and-unique-struggles/402638); [GuitarTricks forum](https://www.guitartricks.com/forum/t/58475)). Musicians who learned from notation keep thinking in written notes at the keyboard ([Sound on Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/people/sounding-david-greeves)). Classically trained musicians find playing melodies by ear harder than musicians from other backgrounds ([score-dependency thesis](https://unipub.uni-graz.at/download/pdf/5757242.pdf); [Woody & Lehmann 2010](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/240725673_Student_Musicians%27_Ear-Playing_Ability_as_a_Function_of_Vernacular_Music_Experiences)).

For a learner whose goals are jamming and learning by ear, the piano path should be shorter in explanations but not in drills, and if anything longer in ear-to-guitar work. Practitioner advice converges on one working rule: shapes are the quickest way to find a chord, but you need to know the notes too ([JazzGuitar.be](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/getting-started/96667-shapes-vs-notes.html)). In app terms, that becomes "spell it, see it on the strip, play it as a shape".

The bridges use what the pianist already knows. Standard tuning makes each string a one-row chromatic keyboard: one fret is one key, black keys included. The strings sit five keys apart, except G and B, which are four apart. Middle C is the B string at the 1st fret. One pitch can appear in up to five places on a 20-fret neck, against exactly one key on a piano. Two devices turn the keyboard map into scaffolding for the new one. One is Riffhard's advice to play the C major scale along one string before crossing strings ([Riffhard](https://www.riffhard.com/?p=37363)). The other is a linked display: tap a key on the strip and every matching fret lights up.

Intervals, the pianist's native unit, become fret counts: 3, 4, 5 and 7 frets along one string. The shape is the same on every pair of strings except where the higher string is the B ([FaChords](https://www.fachords.com/fretboard-intervals/)). Chord spelling becomes guitar voicing once the learner sees that open G (320003) spells G–B–D–G–B–G: three roots and two thirds, nothing like a bass note plus close triad. That is why a Guitar Player lesson has guitarists play their open chords on the keys ([Guitar Player](https://guitarplayer.com/lessons/grab-your-acoustic-sit-down-at-the-piano-and-advance-your-fretboard-logic)). Triads on the top three strings, in their three inversions, are the guitar voicing closest to a pianist's right hand, so they can come early. CAGED can then be framed as five voicings of one triad.

The number system costs a pianist one lesson; what is new is its fixed geometry on the neck. With E-shape and A-shape barre chords, I sits on the 6th string at fret n, IV on the 5th string at the same fret, and V on the 5th string two frets higher. Shapes slide unchanged to any key, the transposing "superpower" one pianist-turned-guitarist describes ([KVR forum](https://www.kvraudio.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=7525528)). Two orientation points need explicit teaching. The "high" E string is the one nearest the floor. And a chord's shape name under a capo (G shapes, capo 2) differs from the key that sounds (A), so the strip should always show the sounding pitch.

| Layer | Transfers from piano? | Evidence | Path decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concepts: note names, intervals, spelling, keys, function, inversions | Yes | Skilled pianists carry musical structure over to new movements ([Palmer & Meyer 2000](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/1467-9280.00216)) | Test out, with one-screen "how it looks on guitar" summaries; number system in one lesson |
| Fretboard map: where pitches live, duplicate locations, shapes | No, though the keyboard map can scaffold it | The left-to-right pitch map is shaped by the instrument played ([Lega et al. 2020](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/338751880_Instrumental_expertise_and_musical_timbre_modulate_the_spatial_representation_of_pitch)); finding notes is pianists' most reported struggle | Keep every note-finding drill with full speed targets, framed as "six keyboards", plus keyboard-to-fretboard translation drills |
| Ear to hand: hearing a sound and finding it on the instrument | No | Links between sound and action are specific to each instrument ([Drost et al. 2007](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17470210601154388)) | Keep and lengthen ear-to-guitar drills, even for strong piano ears |
| Technique and role: fretting, picking, boom-chuck, the guitar's rhythm-section job | No | Fretting pressure and calluses are new ([SWMRS](https://swmrs.com/how-hard-is-learning-guitar-after-piano/)) | Technique from day one, alongside the fast-tracked theory |

Placement should measure two things separately. A graded question decides who is offered theory test-outs, with four answers: "a little", "I read and play pieces", "I know scales, chords and keys", and "I play from chord charts and think in numbers". The grading matters because "plays piano" covers everyone from a method-book reader to a church pianist who reads chord charts. The theory test runs off the guitar, on the keyboard strip. The fretboard-and-technique test runs on the guitar and is timed. It includes translation items such as "this key lights up; play it on the 4th string".

A high theory score must never place a learner past note-finding, chord changes in time, boom-chuck, picking or ear-to-guitar stages. Those stages keep full mastery and speed targets, with shorter explanations in keyboard terms. Technique runs from day one alongside the fast-tracked theory, so the gap between knowledge and hands narrows rather than widens.

The strip should be on by default for new material. During recall drills it should be hidden until after the answer, so the learner shows that accuracy holds without it. The Fretlight result supports this kind of visual guidance in general ([Keebler et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4034341)), though no study has tested fading a keyboard-to-fretboard display. One encouraging finding concerns older adult pianists. In a study of learning motor sequences, unrelated to guitar, piano experience went with faster learning during practice and better consolidation afterwards, and the consolidation benefit came from older participants ([PMC4917083](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4917083/)).

## Conclusion

The research changes the question to ask of every lesson. Instead of "what theory does this teach?", ask "what single sound will the learner play to prove they heard it?" The answers are the tonic after a cadence, the root on beat 1, the third of the current chord, a phrase echoed back and a skeleton break over boom-chuck. Each is at once theory, ear training, fretboard practice and jam preparation, and each is a task a single-note detector scores well. That overlap is Fretwise's strategic edge. Rival ear trainers mostly drill labels, while the evidence puts the bottleneck for these goals in linking ear to hand on the learner's own instrument. The same view shows why a pianist's head start is real but narrow: they can name almost everything, yet play little of it by ear on the neck.

Fretwise can also generate the evidence this field lacks. Guitar-skill research is so thin that a 14-person pilot is the only controlled comparison of fretboard-memorization methods. Nothing compares CAGED with triads, 1-5-3 with 1-2-3, or a cadence with a drone. An app that logs, for every item and across many learners, the accuracy, the response time and the result of the delayed re-check can settle such questions with A/B tests. Those tests should be judged on delayed retention and on unseen songs, not on scores within a session. The sequence here is best treated as version one of that experiment: defensible today, and built so its weakest assumptions can be replaced by Fretwise's own data. Those assumptions are the thresholds, the spacing gaps, and the order within the triad and off-chord phases.
